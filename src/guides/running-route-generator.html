Most runners have two or three loops they run on autopilot. That's not a planning failure, it's just what happens: after a long day, the route you know beats the route you'd have to think about. The trouble is that running the same streets every time is how running gets boring, and boring is how running stops.

Here's every practical way to find a new running route, from fully manual to fully automatic.

Option 1: Trace a route on a map before you go

The classic approach: open a map tool, click out a loop, check the distance, and memorize it (or print it, if you're feeling retro). Tools like Strava's route builder or plotaroute.com work well for this, and they're a good fit when you're training for a race and need a specific distance with specific elevation.

There are real downsides, though. It takes five to fifteen minutes per route, and you have to do it before you're standing outside in your shoes. And because drawing routes is work, most people end up reusing the ones they've already made, which lands you right back in the same-three-loops problem.

Option 2: Follow someone else's route

Strava heatmaps and segment explorers show where other people run in your area. This is genuinely useful for finding the popular waterfront path or the park loop everyone seems to know but you.

The catch is that popular routes are popular with everyone, so you end up in the city's rut instead of your own. Following an unfamiliar route from a phone screen also means stopping at corners to check the map, which turns a run into a navigation exercise.

Option 3: Just run and improvise

Turn left whenever you feel like it. It's free, spontaneous, and occasionally great. It's also how you end up four kilometres from home on what was supposed to be a thirty-minute run, walking back along a road with no sidewalk. Improvising works well when you have unlimited time; the rest of the time it's a gamble.

Option 4: Use a running route generator

This is the category we built Random Run for. A route generator flips the work around: instead of drawing a route yourself, you say how far or how long you want to run, and the app builds loop routes that start and end exactly where you're standing.

What to look for in a route generator, whichever one you choose:

  • Loops, not out-and-backs. A good generated route should bring you home without retracing your steps.
  • Multiple options per request. Seeing three routes and picking one beats accepting whatever the algorithm spits out first.
  • Turn-by-turn guidance. A route you've never run only works if you don't have to stop and study the map. Voice guidance is the difference between running a new route and navigating it.
  • Time-based targets. "30 minutes" is a more honest goal than "5 km", especially if the app knows your real pace.
  • Watch support. If you run with an Apple Watch, a standalone watch app means the phone stays home.

Why new routes matter more than runners think

Variety isn't just about boredom. Different streets mean different surfaces, turns, and small hills, which spreads the load across your legs in a way that one memorized loop doesn't. Exploring on foot is also the fastest way to learn a neighbourhood; runners who vary their routes tend to know their city better than almost anyone.

The best running route is one you've never run. There are hundreds of them within a kilometre of your front door.
